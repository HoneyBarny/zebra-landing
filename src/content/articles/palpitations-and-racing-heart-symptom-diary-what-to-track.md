Palpitations can feel like a racing heart, pounding, fluttering, skipped beats, flip-flops, or a sudden awareness of your heartbeat. For people with [POTS](/knowledge/pots/), [dysautonomia](/knowledge/dysautonomia/), orthostatic intolerance, anxiety overlap, medication changes, thyroid questions, Long COVID, or chronic illness flares, the hard part is often explaining the episode after it has passed.

A palpitations diary does not diagnose an arrhythmia, POTS, dysautonomia, or any heart condition. It helps you bring a clearer episode history to the clinician who can decide what needs evaluation.

## Start With The Episode Basics

A useful note starts with what happened and when.

Track:

- start time
- end time or total duration
- what it felt like: racing, pounding, fluttering, skipped beats, irregular, forceful
- whether it started suddenly or gradually
- whether it stopped suddenly or gradually
- what you were doing when it started
- whether you were lying, sitting, standing, walking, exercising, showering, eating, or resting
- what helped it settle

Example: "Started 2:15 p.m. while standing in kitchen. Heart felt fast and pounding, HR 132 standing, lightheaded 6/10. Sat down, drank water, improved by 2:35."

That gives your clinician a sequence, not just "my heart was weird."

## Add Heart Rate And Position If You Already Measure

If heart rate tracking is part of your care plan, note the number with the position.

Useful labels:

- lying
- sitting
- standing
- after standing for 2, 5, or 10 minutes
- after stairs
- after shower
- after meal
- after exercise
- resting in bed

The same heart rate can mean different things depending on posture and context. A racing heart after sprinting is different from a racing heart while lying down.

Do not start checking constantly if it makes symptoms or anxiety worse. Ask your clinician what frequency and format are useful.

## Track Symptoms That Happened With It

Palpitations are more clinically useful when the connected symptoms stay visible.

Track whether you had:

- [dizziness](/knowledge/dizziness/)
- [lightheadedness](/knowledge/lightheadedness/)
- near-fainting or [syncope](/knowledge/syncope/)
- chest pain, pressure, or tightness
- shortness of breath
- sweating
- nausea
- tremor or shakiness
- headache
- weakness
- blurry vision
- brain fog
- unusual fatigue afterward
- panic feeling or adrenaline surge

Seek urgent medical help for chest pain or pressure, fainting, severe shortness of breath, severe dizziness, confusion, or a racing or irregular heartbeat that does not resolve quickly.

## Keep Possible Triggers Nearby

Palpitations can have many possible contributors. A tracker is most useful when it records context without jumping to a conclusion.

Possible context:

- standing up
- prolonged standing
- heat or hot shower
- exercise or exertion
- dehydration or missed fluids
- skipped meal
- large meal
- caffeine
- alcohol
- nicotine
- new medication or supplement
- missed dose
- stress or strong emotion
- poor sleep
- fever, infection, or illness
- menstrual cycle or hormone change

Write "possible trigger" rather than "cause" unless a clinician has confirmed the relationship.

## Record Medication And Stimulant Context

Clinicians often ask what you take and when, including prescribed medications, over-the-counter medicines, supplements, caffeine, nicotine, and other stimulants.

Track:

- daily medication timing
- missed doses
- dose changes
- new medication or supplement
- decongestants or cold medicines, if relevant
- caffeine timing and amount
- alcohol, if relevant
- nicotine or other stimulant exposure
- whether symptoms changed after a medication change

Do not stop or adjust medication based on a diary alone. Use the record to ask safer, more specific questions.

## Add Hydration, Salt, And Recovery

For POTS and dysautonomia, palpitations often overlap with [hydration](/knowledge/hydration/), [salt](/knowledge/salt-loading/), posture, and heat.

Record:

- water or electrolytes that day
- salt intake if your clinician recommends it
- whether you wore compression garments
- whether lying down helped
- whether sitting helped
- whether cooling helped
- how long recovery took
- whether fatigue, brain fog, nausea, or shakiness lingered

Recovery matters. A 5-minute palpitation episode with two hours of fatigue may be more important than the episode length alone.

## Summarize Function, Not Just Numbers

Write down what the episode changed.

Examples:

- had to sit on the floor
- could not finish shower
- stopped cooking
- left store
- missed class
- could not drive
- canceled exercise
- needed help
- felt unsafe standing
- recovered and continued normally

Functional impact helps your clinician understand daily-life severity.

## What To Bring To The Appointment

Before a cardiology, neurology, primary care, dysautonomia, Long COVID, or EDS appointment, summarize:

- how often palpitations happen
- average duration
- longest episode
- whether they happen at rest, standing, after meals, during showers, with heat, or with exertion
- highest heart rate if measured
- whether rhythm felt regular or irregular
- symptoms that came with the episode
- medication, caffeine, hydration, and salt context
- what helps them settle
- functional impact
- any urgent symptoms: chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, severe dizziness, or confusion

Helpful questions:

- "Do these episodes need ECG, Holter monitoring, or other testing?"
- "What symptoms should be urgent?"
- "Should I record heart rate, blood pressure, or position during episodes?"
- "Could medication timing, caffeine, hydration, salt, heat, or standing be relevant?"
- "How many episodes should prompt follow-up?"

## Key Takeaways

- Track timing, rhythm feeling, heart rate if measured, posture, symptoms, triggers, medications, hydration, and recovery together.
- Palpitations with chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, or severe dizziness need urgent care.
- Position matters: lying, sitting, standing, showering, and exertion tell different stories.
- A short, consistent episode diary can make cardiology or dysautonomia visits easier.
