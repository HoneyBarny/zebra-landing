Hydration, salt, and compression garments are often tracked as separate habits, but they are more useful when they stay connected to symptoms, upright tolerance, and flare history. The point is not to prove what caused a better or worse day. It is to keep enough context to review what changed before an appointment.

For people tracking [POTS](/knowledge/pots/), [dysautonomia](/knowledge/dysautonomia/), or [orthostatic intolerance](/knowledge/orthostatic-intolerance/), support routines can matter because symptoms often shift with position, activity, fluids, heat, sleep, and daily stress. A simple record can help you discuss those shifts without rebuilding the whole month from memory.

## What to track together

Keep the categories small:

- hydration or fluid intake
- salt or electrolyte support if relevant to your routine
- compression garments or support garments
- upright symptoms
- flare severity
- function impact
- medication or routine changes

You do not need a perfect wellness log. You need a record that is clear enough to scan later.

## Why these details belong beside symptoms

Hydration, salt, and compression garments are only useful in a history when they have context.

For example:

- Did dizziness worsen on a day you skipped a usual support?
- Did upright tolerance change during a flare?
- Did symptoms shift when heat, sleep, or activity also changed?
- Did you wear compression garments during the period you are trying to summarize?

These notes do not prove cause. They give your care team a clearer picture of what happened around the symptoms.

## How to avoid overtracking

Use broad labels instead of exact numbers when exact numbers are too much.

Examples:

- fluids: low, usual, higher than usual
- salt/electrolytes: none, usual, extra
- compression: not worn, partial day, most of day
- upright symptoms: mild, moderate, hard day

That level of detail is often enough for appointment preparation.

## When to add more detail

Add detail when something changes.

Useful moments to note:

- a new support garment
- a change in salt or fluids
- a stronger flare
- a day with unusual dizziness or palpitations
- a day when daily function dropped
- a medication or routine change

Changes usually matter more than perfect totals.

## How to review it before an appointment

Before a visit, look for:

- days when upright symptoms were worse
- support routines that changed around those days
- repeated patterns
- questions you want to ask
- anything you do not want to forget

The goal is a short appointment summary, not a long spreadsheet.

## Key takeaways

- Track hydration, salt, and compression garments beside symptoms when they are part of your care context.
- Rough, consistent categories are often more sustainable than exact numbers.
- Use the record to prepare better questions, not to diagnose yourself.

## FAQ

### Should I track hydration, salt, and compression garments together?

If they are part of your routine or care conversations, tracking them beside symptoms can make the record easier to review.

### Do I need exact hydration or salt numbers every day?

Not always. A consistent rough record of changes, missed days, or unusual days can still be useful.

### Can tracking compression garments show whether they work?

Tracking can show context and patterns to discuss with a clinician, but it should not be treated as proof or medical advice.
