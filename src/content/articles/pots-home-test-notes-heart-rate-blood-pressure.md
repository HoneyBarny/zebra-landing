Home notes for [POTS](/knowledge/pots/) or [orthostatic intolerance](/knowledge/orthostatic-intolerance/) are most useful when heart rate and blood pressure stay connected to posture, timing, symptoms, and context.

If you already record lying, sitting, or standing measurements, write down the setup, each time point, symptoms at that same moment, what helped you recover, and what the episode changed in your day. The goal is not to diagnose yourself at home. The goal is to bring clearer observations to your clinician.

## Home notes are not a medical test result

Clinical sources describe active standing tests and tilt table tests as part of a clinician-led evaluation. Home measurements can be useful context when your healthcare team asks for them, but they can also be incomplete or unreliable. Devices vary, symptoms can change fast, and several conditions can affect heart rate or blood pressure.

Write home notes as observations:

- "standing minute 2, dizzy, HR 112, BP 110/72"
- "lying down helped after 4 minutes"
- "I had taken morning medication"
- "Can we review what this means?"

Do not write them as a diagnosis.

## Start with safety

Do not do a home standing check if your clinician has told you not to, if you are alone and prone to fainting, or if you feel unsafe.

Stop and sit or lie down if you feel:

- like you may faint
- chest pain
- trouble breathing
- severe weakness
- new neurologic symptoms
- symptoms that feel unusual or severe for you

Home tracking should never be more important than staying safe.

## Record the setup before the numbers

The setup explains why two days can look different.

Write down:

- date and time
- whether it was morning, afternoon, or evening
- how long you had been lying or sitting before starting
- whether you had eaten recently
- hydration and electrolyte context
- salt context if your clinician tracks that with you
- caffeine or alcohol if relevant
- compression garment use
- medication timing, missed doses, or recent dose changes
- illness, period, pain spike, poor sleep, heat, or flare day

The Heart Rhythm Society consensus statement notes that POTS symptoms happen with standing and commonly include lightheadedness, palpitations, tremor, weakness, blurred vision, exercise intolerance, and fatigue. Context helps your clinician decide what the numbers mean.

## Use posture plus time

For each reading, connect the number to posture and time.

Useful labels include:

- lying baseline after quiet rest
- sitting baseline if lying is not possible
- standing immediately
- standing minute 2
- standing minute 5
- standing minute 10, only if safe and clinician-approved
- recovery after sitting or lying down

Consensus and clinical sources describe orthostatic testing with a quiet lying baseline, then repeated upright measurements. The exact format belongs with your clinician. If your care team asked for a specific timing sequence, use their instructions rather than a generic internet template.

## Record heart rate and blood pressure together

Heart rate without blood pressure can miss part of the story. Blood pressure without symptoms can also be hard to interpret.

For each time point, record:

- heart rate
- systolic blood pressure
- diastolic blood pressure
- symptom severity from 0 to 10
- the main symptom at that moment

Example:

| Time | Position | HR | BP | Symptoms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Baseline | Lying | 72 | 112/70 | 1/10, calm |
| Minute 2 | Standing | 112 | 110/72 | 5/10 dizzy, shaky |
| Minute 5 | Standing | 123 | 108/70 | 7/10 palpitations, fog |
| Recovery | Lying | 82 | 114/72 | improving after 4 minutes |

This kind of note is much easier to review than a list of disconnected readings.

## Add symptoms at the same moment

Orthostatic notes are about how the body responds to posture, not just what the monitor says.

Track symptoms like:

- dizziness or lightheadedness
- palpitations or racing heart
- tremor or shakiness
- weakness
- blurred vision or tunnel vision
- nausea
- shortness of breath
- chest discomfort
- sweating, flushing, or feeling hot
- brain fog
- fatigue
- pre-syncope or fainting

If symptoms improve after lying down, write that down too.

## Do not turn criteria into a self-diagnosis

Major consensus sources describe POTS using a sustained heart rate increase with standing, symptoms of orthostatic intolerance, and no significant orthostatic hypotension. They also emphasize clinical interpretation and excluding other causes such as dehydration, infection, anemia, fever, pain, thyroid disease, medication effects, or prolonged bed rest.

So your home note should not say "I diagnosed myself."

It can say:

"I tracked several standing episodes. Symptoms appeared while upright, heart rate increased, and symptoms improved after lying down. Can we review whether this fits POTS, orthostatic hypotension, vasovagal syncope, another dysautonomia pattern, or something else?"

That is a stronger appointment question.

## Track real-life episodes too

Formal notes are useful, but daily-life episodes matter.

Record when symptoms happen during:

- showering
- standing in line
- cooking
- stairs
- errands
- heat exposure
- after meals
- after exercise
- during a flare
- after poor sleep

For each episode, write:

- what you were doing
- how long you were upright
- symptoms
- whether sitting helped
- whether lying down helped
- recovery time
- what the episode changed in your day

## A simple home note template

Use this when you want a structured note:

1. Setup: time, sleep, food, fluids, salt, meds, compression, illness, heat.
2. Baseline: posture, HR, BP, symptoms.
3. Standing readings: minute, HR, BP, symptoms.
4. Stop reason: finished, symptoms, safety, faint feeling.
5. Recovery: sitting or lying, time to improve.
6. Impact: what you could not do after.
7. Question for clinician: what should I track next?

Short, consistent notes are more useful than trying to collect perfect data once.

## What to bring to the appointment

Bring a short summary, not every raw reading.

Useful appointment notes include:

- the clearest two or three examples
- highest and lowest readings if your clinician asked for them
- symptoms that appeared while upright
- what helped you recover
- medication or hydration context
- what changed in daily life
- the questions you want answered next

## Key takeaways

- Record posture, time, heart rate, blood pressure, symptoms, and context together.
- Stop any home check if you feel unsafe.
- Do not change salt, fluids, compression, or medication based on home numbers without your clinician.
- Daily-life episodes are just as important as structured readings.
- The best output is a clear appointment question, not a self-diagnosis.

## FAQ

### What should I record during a POTS home test?

Record the setup, posture, time point, heart rate, blood pressure, symptoms, whether sitting or lying down helped, medication context, hydration or salt if relevant to your care plan, safety notes, and the question you want to ask your clinician.

### Can a home standing test diagnose POTS?

No. Home notes can support a clinician conversation, but POTS diagnosis requires clinical interpretation and ruling out other causes of tachycardia or orthostatic symptoms.

### When should I stop a home orthostatic check?

Stop if you feel unsafe, faint, develop severe symptoms, or your clinician told you to stop under certain conditions. Sit or lie down and seek medical help when symptoms are concerning.

### Should I change salt, fluids, or medication based on home numbers?

No. Do not change treatment based on home readings alone. Use the notes to ask your clinician what changes are appropriate for you.
