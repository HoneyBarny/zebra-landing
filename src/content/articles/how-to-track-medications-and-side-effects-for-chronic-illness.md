When you are managing several medications and supplements at once, the question that actually matters is simple: is this helping, is this causing something new, or is it too soon to tell. A short, consistent tracked history of doses, changes, and what followed is usually enough to answer that, without turning medication tracking into a spreadsheet project.

For people managing POTS, dysautonomia, EDS, fibromyalgia, or overlapping conditions, medication regimens are often complex and change more than once a year. Without a tracked history, it becomes hard to remember which change lined up with which new symptom, or whether something that felt like a side effect three months ago was ever mentioned to a clinician at all.

## Why medication tracking is different from symptom tracking

Symptoms tell you what your body is doing. Medication tracking tells you what changed around it. The value comes from keeping both close together, with dates, so a pattern like "this started two days after the dose increase" is something you can actually see later instead of vaguely remembering.

This matters for three common situations:

1. **A new symptom after starting or changing a medication.** Is it a side effect, a coincidence, or an unrelated symptom flare?
2. **An unclear response to treatment.** Is a medication working, not working yet, or not working at all?
3. **A repeated conversation with a clinician.** Explaining your current regimen and its history from memory, every visit, is tiring and easy to get wrong.

## What to track for each medication

Keep the tracking proportional. A stable, unchanged medication does not need daily attention. A change does.

### 1. What it is and the current dose

Name, dose, and frequency, for prescriptions, over-the-counter medicine, and supplements alike.

### 2. When something changes

Start date for a new medication, and the date of any dose change, whether that is an increase, a decrease, or stopping altogether.

### 3. What followed, in the days after

Note anything new in the days after a change, whether it looks like a side effect or simply feels different. Include the timing, since some effects appear within hours and others take days.

### 4. As-needed (PRN) use

For medications you take only when needed, note the date, the reason, and roughly what happened afterward. A single use tells you little. A pattern over several uses tells you whether it reliably helps.

### 5. What it seems to be doing, described carefully

"Symptoms seem somewhat improved since the increase" is a useful, honest observation. "This medication fixed it" or "this medication is definitely causing X" overstates what personal tracking can actually confirm, especially when other things are also changing at the same time.

## How to tell a side effect from a symptom flare

This is often the hardest distinction, and there is no perfect rule. A few things help:

- **Timing relative to the change.** Something that appeared in the days right after a new medication or dose change is worth noting as a possible side effect, even if you are not certain.
- **Whether it resembles your usual symptoms.** A new type of symptom you have not had before is different from a familiar flare that happens to occur around the same time.
- **Whether it repeats with continued use, or fades.** A true side effect often persists or recurs, while an unrelated flare tends to follow its own separate pattern.

None of this needs to be resolved by you alone. The goal of tracking is to bring a clear, dated account to a clinician, not to diagnose the cause yourself.

## A simple structure to use

If you are short on energy, this is enough:

### Current medications

- [Name, dose, frequency] for each one, updated only when something changes

### Recent changes

- [Date]: started/changed/stopped [medication], new dose if relevant
- What followed: [anything new, with rough timing]

### As-needed medications

- [Date]: took [medication] for [reason]. Result: [helped, no change, unclear]

This is enough structure to answer "what changed and what happened after" without asking you to track a stable regimen every single day.

## Common mistakes when tracking medications

### Only tracking prescriptions

Supplements, over-the-counter medicine, and PRN use all affect the picture. Leaving them out can hide part of the pattern.

### Not noting the exact date of a change

Without a date, it becomes much harder to line up a new symptom with a medication change later. Even an approximate date is better than none.

### Treating one instance as proof

One good day after starting a medication does not confirm it is working, and one bad day does not confirm a side effect. Look for a pattern across more than one instance before treating it as likely.

### Waiting for the next appointment to write anything down

Details from months ago are hard to recall accurately. A dated note made close to the change is far more reliable than a memory reconstructed later.

## If you have brain fog, use the minimum useful version

On harder days, this can shrink to:

- one line: what changed today, if anything
- one line: anything new since then

That is still enough to preserve the timing, which is the part that is hardest to reconstruct from memory later.

## Key takeaways

- Keep medication changes and symptoms in the same dated timeline, not separate lists.
- A stable, unchanged medication does not need daily tracking; a change does.
- Note timing carefully. It is often what makes a pattern visible later.
- Track PRN medication use over time, not just once, to see whether it reliably helps.
- Describe what you notice as an observation, not a confirmed cause or effect.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a symptom and a side effect?

A symptom is part of your underlying condition. A side effect is something that appears to follow a medication or dose change. Tracking both, with dates attached, is what makes it possible to tell them apart over time, rather than guessing in the moment.

### How soon after starting a medication should I start tracking?

Start on day one. The days right after a change are often the most useful for noticing anything new, even if it takes weeks to know whether the medication is working.

### Can tracking tell me if a medication is causing a side effect?

Tracking can show that something new appeared after a dose or medication change, which is a useful observation to bring to a clinician. It cannot confirm the medication caused it, since other things often change around the same time.

### Should I track supplements and over-the-counter medicine too?

Yes. A clinician reviewing your medications benefits from seeing the full picture, including supplements, over-the-counter products, and as-needed medications, not just prescriptions.

### How do I track an as-needed (PRN) medication?

Note when you took it, why, and roughly how it went afterward. Over time, this shows whether a PRN medication is reliably helping, inconsistently helping, or not doing much.

### What if I am on several medications and cannot track every detail?

Focus on changes: new medications, dose changes, and anything new that followed. You do not need to track a stable, unchanged medication every day to keep a useful history.
