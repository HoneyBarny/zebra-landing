Exercise intolerance can mean very different things for different people with [POTS](/knowledge/pots/), [dysautonomia](/knowledge/dysautonomia/), [orthostatic intolerance](/knowledge/orthostatic-intolerance/), Long COVID, ME/CFS, EDS or HSD overlap, migraine, or chronic illness. For one person, it may be dizziness after a short walk. For another, it may be an elevated heart rate that does not settle, heavy legs after stairs, symptoms after physical therapy, or fatigue that arrives later.

An exercise intolerance symptom tracker does not diagnose POTS, dysautonomia, Long COVID, ME/CFS, heart disease, lung disease, anemia, medication effects, or any other condition. It helps you bring a clearer exertion pattern to a clinician who can evaluate it and guide activity safely.

## Start With The Activity

Write what you actually did, not what you think should count as exercise.

Track:

- walking
- stairs
- grocery shopping
- showering plus dressing
- cooking or cleaning
- physical therapy
- stretching
- recumbent bike, rowing, swimming, or floor exercise
- upright exercise
- carrying bags or laundry
- time on your feet
- total duration
- intensity: easy, moderate, hard, or too much

Example: "10 minutes slow walk outside, mild hill, warm weather. HR peaked 132, dizziness 5/10, legs heavy. Sat 15 minutes; fatigue lasted until evening."

That is more useful than "exercise was bad."

## Separate Position And Intensity

For POTS and orthostatic intolerance, body position matters.

Note whether activity was:

- lying down
- reclined
- seated
- standing
- walking
- arms overhead
- bending or squatting
- upright for a long time

Also separate effort from position. A short upright task may trigger more symptoms than a longer seated task. That distinction can help your clinician or physical therapist understand what is happening.

## Track Symptoms During And After Activity

Exercise intolerance notes should show timing.

Record:

- symptoms before starting
- first symptom during activity
- worst symptom
- symptoms immediately after
- symptoms one to three hours later
- symptoms the next day
- what helped
- how long recovery took

Track symptoms such as:

- [dizziness](/knowledge/dizziness/)
- [lightheadedness](/knowledge/lightheadedness/)
- near-fainting or [syncope](/knowledge/syncope/)
- racing pulse or [tachycardia](/knowledge/tachycardia/)
- [palpitations](/knowledge/palpitations/)
- shortness of breath
- chest discomfort
- shakiness
- nausea
- headache
- sweating
- leg heaviness
- weakness
- [brain fog](/knowledge/brain-fog/)
- [fatigue](/knowledge/fatigue/)

If symptoms are severe, new, one-sided, or different from your usual pattern, follow your clinician's guidance about urgent care.

## Add Heart Rate Or Blood Pressure Only If It Is Already Part Of Your Plan

Some people are asked to track heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen level, or recovery after exertion. Others are not.

If you already measure, write:

- resting value before activity
- peak value
- body position
- value after stopping
- how long it took to settle
- symptoms at each point

Do not start intensive monitoring if it makes symptoms or anxiety worse. A plain note such as "heart racing did not settle for 30 minutes" can still help.

## Add Hydration, Salt, Food, Heat, And Sleep

Activity tolerance can change with context.

Useful notes:

- fluids before activity
- electrolytes
- salt if recommended by your clinician
- skipped meal
- large meal
- caffeine or alcohol
- hot room or hot day
- illness or infection
- poor sleep
- period timing
- medication timing
- compression garments

Do not change fluids, salt, medication, supplements, compression, or activity plans based on a tracker alone. Use the pattern to ask better questions.

## Watch For Delayed Worsening

Some people feel worse later, not during the activity.

Track whether you had:

- fatigue later that day
- next-day worsening
- headache or migraine
- flu-like feeling
- sore throat
- swollen glands
- muscle pain
- worse brain fog
- reduced ability to stand, shower, cook, work, study, or socialize

If you have ME/CFS, Long COVID, or possible post-exertional malaise, ask your clinician how to pace activity safely. A tracker should support pacing, not pressure.

## What To Summarize Before A Doctor Visit

Before a primary care, cardiology, neurology, rehabilitation, Long COVID, EDS, or dysautonomia appointment, summarize:

- which activities trigger symptoms most often
- whether upright activity is different from seated or reclined activity
- duration and intensity that usually cause symptoms
- heart rate or blood pressure context, if already measured
- symptoms during activity versus delayed symptoms
- what helps recovery
- how long recovery takes
- functional impact
- any fainting, chest pain, severe shortness of breath, oxygen concerns, injury, or new neurological symptoms

Useful questions:

- "What activity level is safe for me right now?"
- "Should I track heart rate, blood pressure, or recovery time?"
- "Would seated, recumbent, aquatic, or physical therapy-guided activity make more sense?"
- "Could delayed worsening change the plan?"
- "What symptoms mean I should stop or seek care?"

## Key Takeaways

- Track activity type, position, duration, intensity, symptoms, hydration, salt, recovery, delayed worsening, and functional impact together.
- Upright activity can be different from seated or reclined activity.
- Delayed symptoms matter, especially when fatigue or brain fog worsens later.
- Use tracking to support safer clinical conversations, not to push through warning signs.
