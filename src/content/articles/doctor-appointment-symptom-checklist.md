A doctor appointment symptom checklist helps you bring the right details without trying to remember your whole health history in the waiting room. The most useful checklist includes your main symptoms, what changed, flare patterns, functional impact, medications, relevant measurements, and the top questions you need answered.

The goal is not to arrive with perfect records. The goal is to make the appointment easier to use.

This matters for chronic illness because symptoms often change slowly, overlap, or blur together during flare days. By the time you sit down with a clinician, it can be hard to remember what happened this week, what happened last month, and what you wanted to ask.

## Quick checklist

Before your appointment, try to bring:

- your top 3 symptoms
- when each symptom started or changed
- what makes symptoms worse or better
- recent flare dates or bad-day patterns
- examples of what symptoms stopped you from doing
- current medicines, supplements, and recent changes
- relevant home measurements, if you track them
- allergies or past medication problems
- your top 3 questions
- the one decision you most want help making

You do not need every detail. You need the details that help the visit move from vague memory to a clearer conversation.

## 1. Main symptoms

Start with the symptoms that are most relevant to the appointment.

For many Zebra users, that may include:

- dizziness or lightheadedness
- fatigue
- palpitations
- brain fog
- pain
- nausea
- shortness of breath
- weakness
- exercise intolerance
- sleep disruption

Choose symptoms that are new, worse, repeated, or affecting daily life most. If you list twenty symptoms, the most important ones can get lost. If you choose three to five, the conversation has a center.

MedlinePlus recommends writing down symptom details before a visit, including what symptoms are, when they appear, how long they have been happening, and whether they have changed. See [Make the most of your doctor visit](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000860.htm).

## 2. What changed

Doctors often need the change, not just the symptom name.

Write down:

- when the symptom started
- whether it became more frequent
- whether severity changed
- whether recovery changed
- whether a new symptom appeared
- whether a routine, medicine, infection, period, travel, heat exposure, sleep change, or activity change happened around the same time

For chronic illness, "I am tired" may be true but hard to act on. "Fatigue changed from mostly evenings to all day after short activity" gives the appointment more context.

## 3. Flare patterns

If symptoms come in waves, add a short flare section.

Useful flare notes include:

- date or week
- strongest symptoms
- severity
- duration
- possible trigger or context
- what helped, if anything
- whether recovery was different from usual

You do not need to prove a trigger. It is enough to say, "These things happened near the flare."

For more detail, [How to Track Medication Changes During a Flare](/blog/how-to-track-medication-changes-during-a-flare/) and [How to Track Salt, Fluids, and Flares](/blog/how-to-track-salt-fluids-and-flares/) cover related patterns.

## 4. Functional impact

Functional impact is the part many people forget, but it can make symptom notes much clearer.

Write down what symptoms changed in real life:

- missed work, school, or caregiving
- needed to lie down after showering
- could not stand in line
- canceled plans
- needed help with meals
- could not drive
- had trouble reading, speaking, concentrating, or answering messages

This helps translate symptoms into daily consequences. A symptom name tells what you felt. Functional impact shows what it cost.

## 5. Medicines, supplements, and recent changes

Bring a current list of:

- prescription medicines
- over-the-counter medicines
- vitamins
- supplements
- dose changes
- start or stop dates
- side effects or concerns
- missed doses if relevant

AHRQ advises patients to bring all medicines, including prescription medicines, non-prescription medicines, vitamins, and dietary or herbal supplements, and to write down questions before an appointment. See [Be More Engaged in Your Healthcare](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/be-engaged/index.html).

If your appointment is about dizziness, palpitations, fatigue, POTS, dysautonomia, EDS, fibromyalgia, Long COVID, or overlapping symptoms, medication and routine context can help the clinician understand what changed around the same period.

## 6. Relevant measurements

Only include measurements that are relevant to the visit.

Examples:

- heart rate
- blood pressure
- orthostatic observations
- temperature
- sleep notes
- weight change
- step count or activity change, if useful

For POTS or dysautonomia-related appointments, position-based heart rate or blood pressure observations may be useful discussion context. For other appointments, measurements may be less important than symptom timing, functional impact, and medication history.

The checklist should help the visit, not bury it in numbers.

## 7. Your top questions

Write the questions before the visit.

AHRQ's patient materials suggest bringing your top questions and asking about medicines, tests, treatment options, side effects, interactions, alternatives, and next steps. See [The 10 Questions You Should Know](https://www.ahrq.gov/questions/10questions.html).

Useful question formats:

- What pattern here seems most important?
- What should I track before the next visit?
- What would make this urgent?
- What are the next steps?
- What are the benefits and risks of this test or treatment?
- Could this interact with anything I already take?
- If symptoms worsen, who should I contact?

If you have more than three questions, mark the most important ones first. Short appointments move quickly.

## Bottom line

A good doctor appointment symptom checklist is short, specific, and easy to review. It should answer what changed, what repeated, what affected daily life, what medicines or routines changed, and what you need help deciding next.

You are not trying to bring a perfect diary. You are trying to bring the clearest starting point for the conversation.

## FAQ

### What symptoms should I write down before a doctor appointment?

Write down the symptoms most relevant to the visit, especially symptoms that are new, worse, frequent, disruptive, or connected to the reason for the appointment.

### What should I bring to a doctor appointment for chronic symptoms?

Bring a short symptom summary, medication and supplement list, recent changes, relevant measurements, major flare notes, functional impact examples, and your top questions.

### How long should my symptom checklist be?

Keep it short enough to scan during the visit. One page or a few organized sections is usually more useful than a long diary.

### Should I include medications and supplements?

Yes. A clear list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, and supplements is useful appointment context.

### Is this checklist medical advice?

No. This checklist is an organization tool for appointment preparation. It does not diagnose, treat, or replace medical care.
