A positive [tilt table test](/knowledge/orthostatic-test/) can leave you with more questions than answers. The useful next step is to track the symptoms, standing context, recovery time, medications, hydration, salt, and daily-life impact that help your clinician understand what the test result looks like outside the lab.

This matters because the test is only one piece of the story. Cleveland Clinic explains that a tilt table test monitors heart rate, blood pressure, and symptoms while your body moves from lying flat to upright. A positive result means your body responded abnormally during the test, but the meaning depends on the pattern and your medical history.

The goal after the test is not to diagnose yourself. The goal is to bring cleaner follow-up notes.

## Start with what the report actually said

Before you track anything new, write down the exact language from the test report or after-visit summary.

Useful details include:

- whether the report mentioned POTS, orthostatic hypotension, vasovagal syncope, presyncope, syncope, or dysautonomia
- what symptoms happened during the test
- heart rate and blood pressure changes, if listed
- how long you were upright
- whether medication was used during the test
- what your clinician told you to do next

If the wording feels confusing, that is normal. Bring the exact wording to your follow-up instead of trying to translate it from memory.

## Track what happens when you are upright

The most useful post-test notes usually connect symptoms to position and time upright.

Track:

- what happened after standing
- how long you were upright before symptoms started
- whether symptoms improved lying down
- whether sitting helped enough or only lying down helped
- whether symptoms happened after showering, heat, meals, errands, or stairs
- whether recovery took minutes, hours, or the rest of the day

For people being evaluated for [POTS](/knowledge/pots/), [dysautonomia](/knowledge/dysautonomia/), or [orthostatic intolerance](/knowledge/orthostatic-intolerance/), this context can matter as much as the symptom names.

## Keep symptoms short but specific

You do not need to write a paragraph every time.

Use a short list:

- dizziness or lightheadedness
- fainting or near-fainting
- palpitations or racing heart
- shakiness
- weakness
- nausea
- brain fog
- fatigue
- chest discomfort
- shortness of breath
- sweating, flushing, or feeling hot

Then add one sentence: "What was I doing when this started?"

That one sentence turns a symptom list into appointment context.

## Track hydration, salt, compression, and medication context

Only make changes your clinician recommends. But if hydration, salt, compression, or medication are already part of your care plan, track them beside symptoms.

Useful notes include:

- water or electrolyte intake
- salt intake if your clinician told you to track it
- compression garment use
- medication start, stop, or dose changes
- missed doses
- new side effects or concerns
- caffeine or alcohol if relevant

The Heart Rhythm Society consensus statement notes that clinical history for suspected POTS should consider modifying factors and possible triggers, including dehydration, heat, alcohol, and exercise. Tracking these as context can make follow-up conversations more precise.

## Add functional impact

Functional impact is often the difference between "I felt dizzy" and "this changed my day."

Write down examples like:

- had to lie down after showering
- could not stand in line
- could not cook or do dishes
- canceled work, school, caregiving, or plans
- needed help getting around
- could not drive
- needed hours to recover after a short outing

Clinicians cannot always see the cost of symptoms unless you bring real-life examples.

## Questions to ask at follow-up

After a positive tilt table test, consider bringing questions like:

- What pattern did the test show?
- Does this fit POTS, orthostatic hypotension, vasovagal syncope, another dysautonomia pattern, or something else?
- Were blood pressure readings important in my result?
- What symptoms should make me seek urgent care?
- What should I track before the next visit?
- Should I track home heart rate or blood pressure, and if so, how?
- Are hydration, salt, compression, exercise, or medication changes part of my plan?
- What would count as improvement?

If you are worried about forgetting questions in the room, put them in your symptom tracker as soon as they come up.

- A positive tilt table test should be interpreted by your clinician.
- After the test, track symptoms with position, timing, recovery, and daily-life impact.

## FAQ

### What should I track after a positive tilt table test?

Track symptoms, standing context, recovery time, medications, hydration, salt, functional impact, and questions for your follow-up appointment.

### Does a positive tilt table test always mean POTS?

No. A positive result means your body responded abnormally during the test, but your clinician interprets the pattern with your symptoms, history, and other data.

### Should I keep tracking heart rate and blood pressure after the test?

If your clinician wants home observations, track them with position, timing, symptoms, and context. Do not change treatment based on numbers alone.

### What symptoms are useful to write down?

Commonly useful symptoms include dizziness, lightheadedness, fainting or near-fainting, palpitations, fatigue, brain fog, nausea, weakness, shakiness, and shortness of breath.
