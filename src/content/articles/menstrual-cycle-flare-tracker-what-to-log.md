For some people, chronic illness symptoms change around the menstrual cycle. Pain may rise. Fatigue may get heavier. [POTS](/knowledge/pots/) or [dysautonomia](/knowledge/dysautonomia/) symptoms may feel worse before or during bleeding. [Brain fog](/knowledge/brain-fog/), GI symptoms, migraine, sleep disruption, mood changes, and medication needs may shift too.

A menstrual cycle flare tracker should not assume hormones explain everything. It should keep cycle timing beside symptoms, context, and functional impact so you can bring a clearer pattern to your clinician.

## Start with cycle timing

The simplest useful entry starts with dates.

Track:

- first day of bleeding
- last day of bleeding
- cycle length, if you know it
- flow: light, medium, heavy, unusual
- spotting or bleeding between periods
- cramps or pelvic pain
- clots, if relevant to your care conversation
- missed, late, or unusually early periods

Mayo Clinic recommends tracking period start dates for several months to learn what is typical for you and noting changes such as flow, pain, bleeding between periods, and mood or behavior changes.

## Add the symptoms that change around the cycle

You do not need to track every symptom. Choose the symptoms that seem to shift repeatedly.

Common useful categories:

- pain: cramps, pelvic pain, joint pain, muscle pain, migraine, headache
- fatigue: heavier fatigue, unrefreshed sleep, needing naps
- autonomic symptoms: dizziness, lightheadedness, palpitations, fast heart rate, faint feeling
- cognitive symptoms: brain fog, word-finding trouble, memory gaps
- GI symptoms: nausea, diarrhea, constipation, bloating, cramping
- sensory sensitivity: light, sound, touch, heat
- mood symptoms: irritability, anxiety, low mood, feeling overwhelmed
- sleep: insomnia, waking, sleeping more, waking unrefreshed

The point is to show what changed, not to create a perfect cycle chart.

## Track before, during, and after

Cycle-related patterns can be missed if you only log the bleeding days.

Try tracking:

- 5 to 7 days before bleeding
- bleeding days
- 2 to 4 days after bleeding starts
- ovulation window, if you already track it and it matters for you

The Office on Women's Health notes that PMS symptoms are usually tracked across a few months and may occur in the days before a period and improve after the period starts. This kind of timing note can help separate a repeated premenstrual pattern from a one-off flare.

## Keep chronic illness context beside cycle context

Cycle timing is one layer, not the whole story.

Also note:

- poor sleep
- illness or infection
- heat exposure
- hydration and salt context, if relevant to your care plan
- activity or overexertion
- travel or routine change
- stress
- medication start, stop, missed dose, or dose change
- migraine
- MCAS-style reaction or allergy-type symptoms
- recent appointment, procedure, or vaccination

Writing "period day 1, poor sleep, heat, dizziness worse" gives your clinician more to work with than "period caused dizziness."

## Add functional impact

Functional impact turns symptom tracking into appointment-ready history.

Track what changed:

- missed work, school, or plans
- could not stand long enough to cook
- needed to lie down after showering
- could not drive
- needed help with childcare or errands
- pain changed walking, stairs, sleep, or concentration
- heavy fatigue stopped normal tasks
- brain fog made forms, messages, or conversations harder

This helps show how much the cycle-related flare affected your life.

## Medication and treatment notes matter

If medications, supplements, hormonal contraception, pain relief, fluids, salt, compression, or as-needed medicines are part of your care conversation, keep them in the same timeline.

Track:

- what you took
- when you took it
- whether it was prescribed, over the counter, or part of your clinician-approved plan
- whether symptoms improved, worsened, or stayed the same
- any side effects or concerns
- questions for your clinician

Do not start, stop, or change medication or hormonal treatment based on tracking alone. Use the notes to ask better questions.

## Watch for symptoms that need care

Seek medical care for symptoms such as:

- very heavy bleeding
- bleeding between periods that is new or concerning
- severe or new pelvic pain
- fainting
- chest pain or trouble breathing
- possible pregnancy concerns
- symptoms that disrupt daily life
- mood symptoms that feel unsafe, including thoughts of self-harm or suicide

WHO notes that worrisome symptoms such as pain, heavy bleeding, depressed or anxious mood, and absent or infrequent menstruation are valid reasons to seek care. Your tracker should make it easier to ask for help, not convince you to wait.

## A simple cycle flare template

Use this when you want a quick note:

1. Cycle day: before period, day 1, day 2, after period, unknown.
2. Period context: flow, cramps, bleeding changes.
3. Main symptoms: top 1 to 3 symptoms.
4. Nearby context: sleep, heat, illness, activity, stress, hydration, meds.
5. Functional impact: what changed today.
6. Action: rest, medication as directed, clinician contact, question for visit.

Short repeated entries are more useful than one perfect note.

## What to summarize before a doctor visit

Bring:

- which cycle days seem hardest
- what symptoms reliably worsen
- whether POTS-like, dizziness, palpitations, fatigue, pain, GI, migraine, or mood symptoms cluster around the cycle
- flow or bleeding changes
- medication or treatment notes
- functional impact examples
- your top questions

Helpful questions:

- "Do these symptoms fit PMS, PMDD, dysmenorrhea, migraine, endometriosis, fibromyalgia flare, POTS worsening, medication effects, anemia risk, or something else to evaluate?"
- "What symptoms are urgent for me?"
- "How many cycles should I keep tracking?"
- "Should I track flow, pain, orthostatic symptoms, or medication timing differently?"
- "Could any treatments or referrals help with this pattern?"

## Key takeaways

- Track cycle timing beside symptoms, not separately from them.
- Record before, during, and after the period if symptoms shift outside bleeding days.
- Treat cycle context as a pattern to discuss, not proof of cause.
- Functional impact makes the note more useful for appointments.
- Seek care for heavy bleeding, severe pain, fainting, urgent symptoms, or severe mood symptoms.

## FAQ

### What should I track if symptoms flare around my period?

Track period start and end dates, flow, pain, fatigue, dizziness, palpitations, brain fog, sleep, GI symptoms, medications, activity, and what changed in daily function.

### How many cycles should I track before a doctor visit?

Several menstrual health resources suggest tracking symptoms across a few cycles when PMS or recurring cycle symptoms are a concern, but your clinician may recommend a different plan.

### Can cycle tracking explain all symptom flares?

No. Cycle timing can be useful context, but symptoms can also change because of sleep, illness, stress, medication changes, hydration, activity, or another condition.

### When should I seek medical care for period-related symptoms?

Seek medical care for heavy bleeding, severe or new pain, fainting, shortness of breath, chest pain, pregnancy concerns, symptoms that disrupt daily life, or severe mood symptoms such as feeling unsafe or suicidal.
