Fibromyalgia pain is often hard to explain in a short appointment. Between brain fog, nerves and a clock on the wall, the details you planned to mention can disappear. A clear, short description helps your clinician understand what you live with.

## Short answer

Describe five things: **where** the pain is, **what it feels like** in your own words, **how strong** it is on a 0 to 10 scale, **when and after what** it happens, and **what it stops you doing**. Write each one as a sentence you can read aloud.

## Why pain is hard to explain in the room

Pain can be real and still hard to put into words. People often remember that a day was bad, but not the location, the quality or how it changed over the week. Stress, fatigue and brain fog make recall harder, especially in front of someone else. Writing the details down before the visit gives you something to read from instead of reconstructing them from memory.

## Five questions to answer

**1. Where is it?** Name the areas, and say whether it is in one place or spreads. "Neck and upper back, and both hips on bad days" is more useful than "everywhere".

**2. What does it feel like?** Use the words that fit. Common descriptions include aching, burning, stabbing, throbbing, tight, heavy, electric, shooting, or dull. If none fit, describe it in your own words.

**3. How strong is it?** Use a 0 to 10 scale, and decide what your own 0, 5 and 10 mean. Use the same scale every time so your clinician can compare days. A note such as "pain 6 out of 10 after sitting for an hour" is clearer than "pain was bad".

**4. When does it happen?** Note the time of day, how long it lasts, and what came before it. Standing, sitting, cold, poor sleep, a busy day, or a long walk can all be part of the pattern. Write down what you noticed, not what you think caused it.

**5. What does it stop you doing?** This is often the most useful part. Examples include showering without a rest, finishing a shift, cooking a meal, sleeping through the night, or getting to an appointment on time. The [functional impact guide](/blog/how-to-track-functional-impact-for-a-doctor-appointment/) explains how to capture this over time.

## Word bank for pain

| What it feels like | Example sentence |
| --- | --- |
| Aching | "A deep ache in my lower back that builds through the afternoon." |
| Burning | "A burning feeling along my left arm when I type." |
| Stabbing or shooting | "Short stabs in my hip when I stand up quickly." |
| Heavy or tight | "My shoulders feel heavy and tight after I carry shopping." |
| Throbbing | "A throbbing pain behind my eyes after screens." |

Choose the words that match your experience. You do not need the most dramatic word.

## A 60-second script for the appointment

Practise this out loud before the visit:

"My main pain is in **[location]**. It feels like **[words]**. On a typical day it is **[number]** out of 10, and on a bad day it reaches **[number]**. It is usually worse after **[activity or time]** and eases when **[what helps]**. Over the last two weeks it has stopped me from **[one or two examples]**."

Add one example from the last week. Specific examples are easier to follow than general summaries.

## What to bring

Bring one page, not a diary. Include:

- the main locations and the words you use for the pain
- your typical and worst pain on your 0 to 10 scale, with dates
- what makes it better or worse, and how often that happens
- what it stopped you doing in the last two weeks
- your questions, written down before the visit

If you track your pain between visits, a short, consistent entry is enough. Missed days do not erase your history.

## What this does not do

A pain description does not prove a diagnosis, measure pain objectively, or tell anyone what treatment to use. Fibromyalgia is assessed by a clinician, who considers your history, examination and other causes. Seek urgent care for sudden severe pain, new weakness or numbness, chest pain, trouble breathing, or any symptom that feels like an emergency.

## Key takeaways

- Describe location, feel, intensity, pattern and daily impact in plain words.
- Use the same 0 to 10 scale every time.
- Describe what pain stops you doing, with a recent example.
- Practise a 60-second script, and bring one page.
- A clear description supports your clinician's assessment. It does not replace it.

## FAQ

### How do I describe fibromyalgia pain to a doctor?

Say where the pain is, what it feels like in your own words, how strong it is on a 0 to 10 scale, when it happens, what makes it better or worse, and what it stops you doing.

### Does describing my pain well change the diagnosis?

No. A clear description helps your clinician understand your experience. Diagnosis and treatment decisions are made by your clinician after an assessment.

### What if I cannot find the right word for my pain?

Describe it the way it feels to you, such as heavy, tight, or burning, and say how it compares with your usual day. Plain words are useful.
