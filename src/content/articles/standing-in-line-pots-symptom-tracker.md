For many people with [POTS](/knowledge/pots/), [dysautonomia](/knowledge/dysautonomia/), [orthostatic intolerance](/knowledge/orthostatic-intolerance/), Long COVID, EDS or HSD overlap, ME/CFS, or chronic illness, standing still can be harder than walking. A checkout line, pharmacy wait, bus stop, concert queue, security line, school hallway, kitchen counter, or clinic waiting room can bring dizziness, racing heart, nausea, shakiness, heavy legs, brain fog, sweating, blood pooling, or near-fainting.

A standing-in-line symptom tracker does not diagnose POTS, dysautonomia, low blood pressure, dehydration, anxiety, or any other condition. It helps you bring a clearer prolonged-standing pattern to a clinician: what the standing situation was, how long it lasted, what symptoms appeared, what helped, and what changed in your day.

## Start With The Standing Situation

The most useful note is often simple: where you were, what you were doing, and how long you were upright.

Track:

- checkout line
- pharmacy, clinic, or appointment wait
- bus, train, airport, or rideshare wait
- shopping
- cooking or washing dishes
- event queue
- school or work hallway
- standing conversation
- standing still versus walking slowly
- total time upright
- whether you could sit down

Example: "Grocery line, standing still 12 minutes after 25 minutes shopping. Warm store. Dizziness 6/10, heart racing, legs heavy. Sat in car with water; improved after 20 minutes."

That kind of note is easier to discuss than "shopping wiped me out."

## Separate Standing Still From Walking

Some people tolerate slow walking better than standing still. Others flare with both.

Useful distinctions:

- walking around store
- standing still in line
- standing and carrying bags
- standing with arms raised
- standing in heat
- standing after a meal
- standing after poor sleep
- standing during period or flare

This helps your clinician see whether the issue is mainly time upright, stillness, heat, exertion, meals, or a combination.

## Track Symptoms And Timing

Write when symptoms started and what changed first.

Track whether you noticed:

- [dizziness](/knowledge/dizziness/)
- [lightheadedness](/knowledge/lightheadedness/)
- near-fainting or [syncope](/knowledge/syncope/)
- racing pulse or [tachycardia](/knowledge/tachycardia/)
- [palpitations](/knowledge/palpitations/)
- nausea
- shakiness
- sweating
- shortness of breath
- chest discomfort
- blurry vision
- muffled hearing
- leg heaviness
- purple, red, or swollen feet
- [brain fog](/knowledge/brain-fog/)
- [fatigue](/knowledge/fatigue/)

If you already measure heart rate or blood pressure as part of your care plan, add position and timing: before standing, while standing, after sitting, or after lying down. Do not measure in public if it is unsafe or makes symptoms worse.

## Add Hydration, Salt, Meals, Heat, And Compression

Standing symptoms can change depending on context.

Useful notes:

- fluids earlier that day
- electrolytes
- salt intake if recommended by your clinician
- skipped meal
- large or carbohydrate-heavy meal
- caffeine or alcohol
- warm room, sun, coat, or crowding
- compression socks, tights, abdominal binder, or none
- medication timing
- recent medication change
- illness or infection

Do not change fluids, salt, compression, or medication based on a tracker alone. Use the pattern to ask better questions.

## Record What Helped

Track what reduced symptoms or helped you finish the task.

Examples:

- sitting down
- lying down later
- elevating legs
- cooling
- drinking water
- electrolytes
- snack
- moving feet or shifting weight
- crossing legs
- compression
- asking for a seat
- leaving the line
- using delivery or pickup next time

The point is not to judge the strategy. The point is to learn what makes daily life safer and more realistic.

## Track Recovery And Functional Impact

Recovery is part of the episode.

Write:

- how long symptoms lasted
- whether you returned to baseline
- whether fatigue, headache, nausea, or brain fog lingered
- whether you drove, cooked, worked, studied, showered, or exercised afterward
- whether you cancelled plans
- whether you needed help from someone else

"Felt better after sitting for 5 minutes" and "lost the rest of the afternoon" are different clinical details.

## What To Summarize Before A Doctor Visit

Before a primary care, cardiology, neurology, dysautonomia, Long COVID, EDS, or rehabilitation appointment, summarize:

- which standing situations trigger symptoms most often
- how many minutes upright usually causes symptoms
- whether standing still is worse than walking
- whether heat, meals, hydration, salt, or compression change the pattern
- heart rate or blood pressure context, if already measured
- symptoms that happen first
- what helps
- recovery time
- functional impact
- any fainting, injury, chest pain, severe shortness of breath, confusion, or new neurological symptoms

Useful questions:

- "Could this be related to orthostatic intolerance or blood pooling?"
- "Should I track heart rate or blood pressure during prolonged standing?"
- "Are compression, fluids, salt, or counter-maneuvers appropriate for me?"
- "What symptoms should be urgent?"
- "Can we talk about practical accommodations for lines, shopping, or transit?"

## Key Takeaways

- Track standing time, stillness versus movement, symptoms, heat, fluids, salt, meals, compression, recovery, and functional impact together.
- Standing still can feel different from walking, so separate those notes when you can.
- Track the episodes that change your day, not every second on your feet.
- A consistent standing-in-line diary can make daily-life limitations easier to explain at appointments.
