You described the dizziness, the joint that slips, the night you couldn't sleep. Then you read the notes, and they mention none of it, or they say something you didn't say. You can fix some of this before the visit, some in the room, and some afterwards.

## Short answer

**Before:** write your top three concerns and hand them over first. **During:** ask, "Could you add that to my notes?" **After:** read your notes. If something is wrong, ask the practice in writing to correct it. If they refuse, you can add a short statement saying you disagree. A clinician's opinion usually stays in the record, but your statement goes with it.

## Why notes miss things

A clinician writes the note for the next clinician, often after the visit and from memory. They summarise. A symptom you mentioned quickly, or one they didn't ask about, can drop out. Sometimes the note records their interpretation, and you see it differently.

None of this means you explained it badly. It means that what you said aloud and what they wrote down can come apart. You can narrow the gap at each step.

## Before the visit: put your priorities in writing

Pick the **three things that matter most** for this appointment. Write each one as a sentence you could read aloud, with when it happens and what it stops you doing. For example: "Dizzy within a minute of standing, most mornings. I sit down to shower."

Hand the list over at the start, or ask to read it out first. Three items fit on a glance; a long list may not get read. The [specialist appointment guide](/blog/prepare-for-a-specialist-appointment-chronic-illness/) and the [appointment symptom checklist](/blog/doctor-appointment-symptom-checklist/) cover the rest of the preparation.

## In the room: ask for it to be noted

When you say something important, check that it lands:

- "Could you add that to my notes?"
- "Can you note that this started in August and has got worse since?"
- "Before we finish, can we check my top three are in the notes?"

If your clinician decides not to order a test or a referral you asked about, you can ask, calmly, for your request and their reason to be written down. "Could you note that I asked about a referral, and why we're not doing it now?" This keeps a record of the decision, and it gives the next clinician the full picture.

## After the visit: read your notes

**In the US**, a federal rule in effect since April 2021 generally requires providers to give you electronic access to your clinical notes, usually through the patient portal, unless an exception applies. Look for "visit notes", "clinical notes" or "after visit summary".

**In the UK**, ask your GP practice or hospital how to see your record. Many offer online access.

When you read the note, sort what you find:

| What you find | What it usually means | What you can do |
|---|---|---|
| A wrong fact (date, medicine, side, symptom you never had) | A factual error | Ask for a correction |
| Something you said that's missing | An incomplete record | Ask for it to be added, with the date you said it |
| An opinion you disagree with | A clinical judgement | Ask for a statement of disagreement to be added |
| A typo that changes nothing | Minor | Usually fine to leave |

## How to ask for a correction

Start with the easy route: a message through the portal or a call to the practice, naming the visit and the line. A message through the portal also leaves you a written copy.

If that doesn't work, put it in writing to the practice's medical records or privacy office. Use their form if they have one. Keep a copy of everything you send and receive.

**Request template:**

> I am asking for a correction to my medical record.
> Date of visit: [date]. Clinician: [name].
> The note says: "[exact words]".
> This is inaccurate / incomplete because: [one or two sentences].
> I ask that it be changed to, or that the record add: "[your wording]".
> Supporting information: [for example, your own dated symptom notes].
> Please confirm in writing what you decide.

## Your rights in the US: HIPAA

Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule (45 CFR 164.526), you have the right to ask a provider or health plan to amend your record:

- They must act on your request **within 60 days**. They can extend once, by up to 30 days, if they tell you in writing why and when they will decide.
- They can **deny** a request, for example if they judge the record already accurate and complete. A denial must be in writing, in plain language, and must explain how to disagree.
- If they deny it, you can submit a **statement of disagreement**. They must link it to the disputed part of your record, and include it, or an accurate summary, whenever they later share that information. They may write a rebuttal and must send you a copy.
- If they **accept**, they must amend the record and tell you. You can name people who received the old information and need the correction.

Some states give you extra rights. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Your rights in the UK: rectification

Under UK GDPR you can ask for **factual inaccuracies** to be corrected. NHS guidance says a clinician's professional opinion, if it was reasonable at the time, is usually not changed, but you can ask for a note of your disagreement to be added. Records are amended rather than deleted, and changes should be signed and dated.

The organisation should respond within **one calendar month**. It can extend by up to two more months for complex requests, if it tells you why within the first month. If it refuses, it must explain why. You can complain to the Information Commissioner's Office.

**Elsewhere**, ask the clinic's records or data-protection office how to request a correction. The rules differ by country.

## What this does not do

- It does not guarantee a change. A provider can keep a note it believes is accurate, and a clinician's opinion usually stays.
- It does not change a diagnosis or a treatment plan. Those come from your clinician.
- It is not legal advice. For a dispute with high stakes, such as insurance or disability, ask a patient advocate or a lawyer.
- If a symptom is new, severe or frightening, seek care first. You can fix the paperwork later.

## Key takeaways

- Write your top three concerns down, and hand them over first.
- Ask, "Could you add that to my notes?"
- Read your notes afterwards. Sort what you find into errors, gaps and opinions.
- Ask in writing for a correction, and keep copies.
- If they say no, add a statement of disagreement. It stays with the record.

## FAQ

### Can I see my doctor's notes?

In the US, a federal rule in effect since April 2021 generally requires providers to give you electronic access to your clinical notes, with some exceptions. In the UK, ask your GP practice or hospital how to see your record. Many offer online access.

### Can medical records be amended?

You can ask. In the US, HIPAA gives you the right to request an amendment, and the provider must act within 60 days. In the UK, you can ask for factual inaccuracies to be corrected. In both, a clinician's professional opinion usually stays, but you can add a statement saying you disagree.

### What if my doctor won't change my notes?

Ask for the refusal in writing, then submit a short statement of disagreement to go with the record. In the UK you can also complain to the Information Commissioner's Office.
